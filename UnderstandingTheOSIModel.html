Understanding The OSI Model


What is the OSI model?


OSI stands for: Open Systems Interconnection

Full name being Open Systems Interconnection Reference Model

OSI is used in IT to describe the process that data takes to traverse our networks.

OSI is not a detailed descriptor of this process, but instead a broad overview. It's more of a reference guide, thus the term "model".

It is best not to get wrapped up in the details.


This is not the OSI protocol suite. Most of the OSI protocols used today are mostly from the TCP/IP suite, but the OSI model works perfectly well for describing them.

However, the OSI model can be used with many protocols, and works perfectly with the TCP/IP protocols that we use.


There are many unique protocols at every layer of the OSI model.

As we step through each layer, there may be 10's or 100's of different protocols unique to that layer.


By becoming familiar with the OSI model, we can converse and problem solve with others in IT in a way that we can all understand.


Taking the first letter from the descriptor of every layer, you get the mnemonic "All People Seem To Need Data Processing", or the other way "Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away"


Layer 7: Application


This is the layer that for application protocols (https, DNS, FTP). Any time we are interacting with an application we are operating at layer 7 of the OSI model.


Common application protocols that would operate on layer 7 of the OSI model are: HTTP and HTTPS, FTP, DNS, POP3, and thousands of other application protocols.


In the real world, this would refer to where you, the end user, are interacting with the application on your device. Any protocol that brings you what you're seeing on the screen.


In short, the application, such as https://mail.google.com


Layer 6: Presentation


Layer 6 is responsible for putting all of this data into a format that the user will eventually see with our human eyes.


This refers to character encoding, application encryption and decryption, and is often combined and discussed in conjunction with the Application Layer.


Layer 6 is the layer that is commonly in operation just prior to us seeing the data on our screens.


In the real world, this would refer to Application Encryption (SSL/TLS).


In short, the presentation, such as SSL encryption


Layer 5: Session


Before we are able to get those packets across the network, we may need to create a session to be able to receive that data. Layer 5 is the session layer responsible for this.


Provides communication management between point A and point B. Anything relating to the initiation of the session, such as stopping the session, restarting the session, or starting the session can be commonly associated with layer 5 communication.


Contains some type of control protocol, or tunnelling information within existing data.


In the real world, this would refer to Control Protocols to start and end different sessions, and Tunnelling Protocols.


In short, Link the presentation to the transport


Layer 4: Transport


Referring to the ability to transport information from one device to another. Sometimes also referred to as the "Post Office" layer, because this is responsible for getting your "letter", or information, from one side of the network to another.


The protocols that are often used and operate in layer 4 of the OSI model are TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) and UDP (User Datagram Protocol). These two protocols are commonly responsible for getting all of the information within our IP packets from one device to the other. In many cases, this involves taking a large amount of data, cutting it into smaller pieces to get it across the network, and then putting those pieces back together on the other side.


In the real world, this would refer to TCP segments, or UDP Datagrams.


In short, TCP/UDP encapsulation


Layer 3: Network


Also often referred to as the "Routing" layer because this is the layer that routers use to determine how to forward traffic; specifically looking at the destination IP (Internet Protocol) address in order to determine what the next hop might be to send traffic traversing the network.


Also the layer that we are able to fragment these packets into multiple pieces, especially if the detination requires smaller frames than what is on our network. So we are able to cut these frames into smaller fragments to send across the network, and put them back together on the destination address on the other side.


Any time we are referring to a problem relating to IP addressing, subnetting masks, or anything about routing, people are probably referring to layer 3.


In the real world, this would refer to IP addresses, the Router, and Packets sent across the network.


In short, IP encapsulation, packets.


Layer 2: Data Link


The fundamental layer that's used to communicate between two devices on the network. Often referred to as the MAC (Media Access Control) address layer because it is commonly associated with the network cards on the devices.


Also often referred to as the "Switching" layer.


In the real world, this would refer to Ethernet frames, MAC address, Extended Unique Identifier (EUI-48, EUI-64), and the Switch.


In short, Ethernet, frames.


Layer 1: Physical


The physics of the network. Signaling, cabling, connectors. This layer isn't about protocols, as it's mostly describing getting a signal from one part of the network to another. When we are referring to a Physical Layer problem, we are referring to the OSI layer 1.


This means we may have a bad cable, a bad fibre, interference on a wireless network, and may require a series of tests to confirm if we are able to get a signal through this hardware.


A lot of the work you do across layer 1 would be loopback tests, testing/replacing cables and fibres, and checking different adapter cards and other devices to confirm the hardware is not the fault in the network.


In the real world, this would refer to cables, fibreoptic connections, and the signal itself.


In short: signals, bits.


Wireshark


If you wanted to drill down a little bit further into the OSI model, you may want to capture some data on your network and view that data inside an application such as Wireshark.


Wireshark has 3 seperate windows that is displaying different views of the same data.


The top window is a frame-by-frame breakdown.


The middle window describes more detail on the data in the selected frame. Each line in this window will move up the OSI layer, from the first line being OSI layer 1 (bytes being captured in that frame), Layer 2 (source and destination MAC address), Layer 3 (source and destination IP addresses), to Layer 4 (the protocols used to transport the data, such as source port and destination port of the traffic that was captured), to layer 5,6,7 (described in the Transport Layer Security line).


And the bottom window we have a hexadecimal and ascii breakdown of the data itself.
